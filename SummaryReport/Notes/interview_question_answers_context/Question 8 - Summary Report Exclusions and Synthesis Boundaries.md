## Contextual Interpretation

The summary report should deliberately avoid becoming a duplicate of the detailed technical reports or expanding beyond its intended advisory and interpretive function.

### Explicit Exclusions

The summary report should avoid:

- repeating detailed findings already documented in the source reports, except where brief reference is necessary to support interpretation;
- reproducing long technical tables, detailed measurements, or granular inspection data;
- becoming a maintenance work instruction;
- becoming a detailed engineering design document;
- assigning formal risk ratings unless those ratings were already established in the source material;
- creating artificial technical or causal links between the three audited areas;
- assigning responsibility or blame to individuals, teams, or departments;
- making commercial, contractual, or legal conclusions;
- promising that a recommended action will fully eliminate a problem;
- presenting conceptual drawings as final engineered design solutions.

Where detailed evidence is required, the summary report should refer the reader back to the relevant source report.

### Remedial Philosophy — Prove Before Scaling

Where remedial actions are recommended, the preferred philosophy is generally:

1. apply the remedial concept to a defined location, section, or subset;
2. verify whether the intended result is achieved;
3. confirm that the approach is effective in practice;
4. only then scale or extend that philosophy to other relevant sections, systems, or components where applicable.

The summary report should therefore avoid language that implies universal effectiveness before the remedial concept has been proven in the intended operating context.

### Higher-Level Synthesis Is Permitted

The prohibition on unsupported new conclusions does **not** prevent the summary report from drawing legitimate higher-level conclusions from combined evidence.

The detailed reports are intentionally granular and section-specific. When several reports are considered together, a system-level pattern, trend, or implication may emerge that is not explicitly stated in any single source report.

A valid summary-level conclusion may therefore be developed where:

- it is supported by the combined evidence from the detailed reports;
- the reasoning is traceable;
- the conclusion does not contradict the source reports;
- the distinction between direct source finding and holistic interpretation remains clear.

For example, the three Cable Belt Sheave reports may collectively reveal a broader condition trend or system-level implication that was not explicitly written into any one of the individual reports because each report focused on a specific sheave section.

## Contextual Significance

This answer establishes a critical synthesis rule:

**The detailed reports are the evidentiary foundation, but the summary report is permitted to derive broader, evidence-based system-level conclusions from their combined context.**

The report must therefore avoid two opposite errors:

1. inventing unsupported conclusions; and
2. refusing to state a legitimate holistic conclusion simply because no single detailed report states it verbatim.

The summary report's value lies partly in this evidence-based synthesis.
